BUSINESS · ESSAY

Software, land
and stocks.

Three roads a young entrepreneur can take to serious wealth, compared honestly, and how I stack them.

I am twenty. I want to reach a scale that people raise an eyebrow at. So I have to be honest about the three vehicles in front of me: software, real estate and stocks. Each one builds wealth in a completely different way, and confusing them is how young people lose money.

Software: the highest ceiling, the fastest clock

A product can serve a hundred customers or a million with roughly the same code. Margins are high, distribution is cheap and the asset keeps working while you sleep. Of the three, it is the only one where a person with no capital can start with nothing but a laptop.

The catch is that software has no intrinsic value. It is worth what customers pay for it, and nobody pays for a demo. Every month you are re-earning the right to exist. That is why I obsess over clients who renew, not launches that trend.

Real estate: the slowest, the most solid, the most borrowed-against

Land does not pivot. It does not need a marketing budget. In Uganda it appreciates, it produces rent, and a bank will lend against it in a way no bank will lend against my code. It is also the asset ordinary families trust, which is exactly why fraud thrives in it.

The catch is liquidity: you cannot sell a plot on a Tuesday to make payroll, and a bad title can destroy a lifetime of savings. That is the reason Sageco Evergreen exists, verification and escrow are the product, not a feature.

Stocks: the most liquid, the least control

Stocks ask the least of my time and give the least of my control. I can enter and exit in minutes, and compounding quietly does the work. For a young person, it is the most forgiving place to learn how capital behaves, because mistakes are cheap and reversible.

The catch is that you own a fraction of decisions other people make. It can build wealth, but it will not build a builder. It is a place for money to wait without sleeping.

The advice I would give another twenty-year-old

  1. Build the engine first. Software or a trade, something that produces cash every month. Assets bought before you have cash flow get sold in a panic.
  2. Never borrow to speculate. Borrowing against land to buy stocks is how promising young people disappear from the story. Leverage belongs on assets that produce income.
  3. Verify before you pay. The cheapest land in Uganda is usually cheap because the title is a problem. A search at the land office costs less than a mistake.
  4. Keep a buffer you never touch. Six months of costs in something liquid. It is the difference between choosing your next move and accepting any move.
  5. Buy assets that pay you, then assets that appreciate. Rent before speculation, dividends before hype.
  6. Compound quietly for twenty years. The people who look like overnight successes were usually boring for a decade. Boredom is the strategy.
  7. Own, do not only earn. A salary buys a lifestyle. Equity buys freedom. Take the smaller salary and the larger share when you can.

How I stack them

Software gives me cash flow and ownership. Real estate gives me security and leverage. Stocks give me liquidity and patience. None of them is a shortcut, and any one of them alone will cap you: a coder with no assets stays a coder, a landlord with no cash flow is trapped, and a trader with no business never learns to build.

Stacked and compounded over twenty years, from Fort Portal, that is how an ambitious young entrepreneur reaches billionaire scale without gambling everything on one bet.

Smart people are doubted at the start. Results do the talking.

This is how I think about building, not financial advice.
